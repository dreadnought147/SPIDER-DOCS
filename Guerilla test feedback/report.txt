COMIC STOCK — GUERILLA TEST REPORT
Phase 1 Checkout Paper Mockup
==========================================

[ PICTURE PLACEHOLDER: paper mockup — home / listing screen ]


WHAT WE DID
-----------
We took our paper mockup of the Comic Stock checkout flow to five
people and asked each of them to walk through buying a comic book
from start to finish. We watched where they got stuck, where they
hesitated, and what they assumed buttons would do. Two of the
testers were technical (a product designer and a developer); the
other three were normal end users — the kind of people who would
actually buy a comic on the site.

The point was simple: find out what works, find out what does not,
and use that to inform the digitized mockup.


WHO WE SPOKE TO
---------------
- Technical User 1 — product designer, gave us feedback on the
  quality of the mockup itself rather than the flow.
- Technical User 2 — developer, focused on UI consistency and
  attention to detail.
- End User 1 — first-time visitor, walked the full purchase flow.
- End User 2 — two friends testing together, gamers, casual buyers.
- End User 3 — experienced online shopper.


[ PICTURE PLACEHOLDER: paper mockup — cart screen ]


WHAT WE LEARNED — THE BIG THEMES
--------------------------------

1. THE CART NEEDS TO BE EDITABLE
Every single end user tried to change something in the cart and
could not. They wanted to increase quantity, remove an item, or
clear the cart entirely. Right now the cart is read-only and that
frustrated all of them. This was the most consistent piece of
feedback we got.

2. THE CHECKOUT ASKS FOR TOO MUCH
Users felt that filling in delivery details, billing info and
preferences during checkout is stressful. As one user put it,
"when you are checking out, your mind is telling you about the
money you are about to spend — you do not want extra stress."
The expectation is that this information should be captured when
the account is created, and then offered as a quick selection at
checkout (saved address, saved payment method).

3. THE CONFIRMATION PAGE REPEATS ITSELF
Users said the order confirmation screen showed them the same
information they had already seen two or three times during
checkout. The preference was clear: keep the on-screen confirmation
short (just "your order is confirmed" plus an order number) and
send the full receipt by email.

4. UNCLEAR HOW TO GET FROM HOME TO CART
End User 1 added a comic to the cart but could not figure out how
to actually open the cart from the home page. The cart icon was
not obvious. Technical User 2 also flagged this — "your cart icon
doesn't look like a cart and is in a weird place."

5. WE ARE OVERPROMISING FEATURES
Both technical and end users pointed at features we had drawn in
(wishlist hearts, similar-item recommendations, reviews) and asked
why they were there if the focus is the checkout. The advice was
the same from multiple people: under-promise and over-deliver.
Take the cool stuff out for now. Ship the basics properly first.

6. MISSING DETAILS THAT ACTUALLY MATTER
- No description of the comic on the listing page. Users wanted a
  short Netflix-style blurb to decide whether to buy.
- No VAT shown on prices (this is a legal requirement in SA).
- No shipping cost shown until very late in the flow.
- "Apple Pay" assumed without offering anything for Android users.

7. THE FLOW ITSELF IS FINE
This is worth saying clearly: nobody said the order of the steps
was wrong. End User 3 said directly, "the flow is fine, it follows
proper e-commerce flow." The structural decisions are good. The
problems are at the level of detail, polish, and what we ask of
the user inside each step.

8. MOCKUP-QUALITY FEEDBACK (from the product designer)
Technical User 1 told us our paper mockup needed more context on
each screen — labels saying what page this is, numbers showing
which step in the flow we're on. Useful for future testing
sessions: decide what information you want out of the user before
showing them anything.

THE RAW VOICE OF THE USERS
--------------------------
We want to capture what people actually said, in their own words.
These are the lines that stuck with us when we listened back.

On the cart being read-only:
  "What if I wanted to remove one?"
  "If I want to delete an item, I'm not so sure about this."
  "I can't edit, and I can't delete from what I see."

On checkout asking for too much:
  "When you're checking out, your mind is telling you about the
   money that you're about to spend. You do not want to have
   extra stress of adding this type of information."
  "I am a little bit annoyed, because now I have to fill in all
   my details."

On the confirmation page repeating itself:
  "I've already seen them three times already."
  "If anything, send an email with full details of everything."

On overpromising features:
  "Under-promise, and over-deliver."
  "Be as basic as possible. Under promise, but over deliver."

On the cart icon being unclear:
  "How do I get to the wishlist? You've got wishlist icons here."
  "Your cart icon, which doesn't look like a cart, in a weird place."
  "Here you have add to cart. Here you have the cart icon.
   Which one is it?"

On missing details:
  "There's no description of the comic."
  "I don't see VAT."
  "You never told me what am I paying for shipping."

On the flow itself:
  "The flow is fine. It follows proper e-commerce flow."
  "As a customer, it's pretty straightforward, not too confusing.
   Just minor things."

On the mockup quality (from the product designer):
  "You could go about improving these low fidelities is a bit
   more context as to what screen I'm looking at."
  "Labelling your pages that speaks to what you're trying to
   visualize."


COMMONALITIES — WHERE EVERYONE AGREED
-------------------------------------
Some pieces of feedback came up from more than one person. When
multiple users say the same thing without prompting, that is the
strongest signal we got.

- Cart needs editing — said by all 3 end users.
- Confirmation page repeats too much — said by 2 end users.
- Cart icon / how to get to cart is unclear — said by 1 end user
  AND the technical developer.
- Don't overpromise cool features — said by 2 testers across
  technical and end users.
- The flow itself is acceptable — said by 2 end users directly.
- Filling details from scratch is annoying — said by 2 end users.


THE OVERALL SENTIMENT
---------------------
The feeling we walked away with was positive but humbling.
Nobody told us we had built the wrong thing. Nobody got
fundamentally lost. The structure of the checkout journey
makes sense to people. What they kept catching us on was the
small stuff — the details, the polish, and the features we
added that we did not need.

If we had to put the sentiment into one sentence: "the bones
are right, the flesh needs work."


WHAT WE CHANGED IN THE DIGITIZED MOCKUP
---------------------------------------
[ PICTURE PLACEHOLDER: digitized mockup — for comparison ]

Based on the feedback above, the digitized version:

- Adds +/- quantity buttons and a remove option in the cart.
- Adds a "clear cart" action.
- Shows a clearer cart icon in a more conventional location.
- Adds a short description to comics on the listing.
- Pulls saved address and saved payment method from the user's
  account at checkout, with the option to edit if needed.
- Trims the confirmation page to a simple confirmation message
  plus order number, with the full receipt going to email.
- Removes the wishlist, reviews and recommendations from Phase 1.
- Shows shipping cost and VAT inside the cart total breakdown,
  not only at the final step.
- Replaces the Apple Pay assumption with a generic payment
  method selector that includes card, EFT, and mobile options.


WHAT WE DID NOT CHANGE — AND WHY
---------------------------------
- The overall step order of the checkout flow stays the same.
  Multiple users confirmed it follows what they expect from
  e-commerce.
- We are keeping the account-based model. Even the user who said
  "filling in details is annoying" assumed they had an account
  already — that confirms the account-based decision rather than
  contradicting it.


CLOSING NOTE
------------
The biggest win from this round of testing is that the structure
of our flow is sound. The problems are fixable details, not
foundational mistakes. The next round of testing should be done
on the digitized mockup, and we should go in with a clearer set
of questions — exactly what the product designer told us at the
start.
